What the Oracle Is

The Demos Network Oracle (DNO) is a public observability layer for the Demos blockchain testnet. It reads the network's public seeds from outside, measures whether the heights they report agree, tracks specific problems, and publishes what it finds.

The Oracle is strictly watch-only. It observes, interprets, and summarizes. It does not command validators, does not advise operators, and does not make predictions about what will happen next. It publishes what it observed and the rules it applied. What anyone does with that is their decision.

The Oracle is independent. It is not operated by the Demos core team. It does not control any downstream system. Any agent, builder, or person can consume its output and form their own conclusions.

DNO also publishes a versioned Criteria Catalog — the definitions of its observation criteria (C1–C6), available as data at /criteria.json.

What It Measures

Every reading is published as seven values. Each answers one question about what DNO observed. Some are built from others: status uses data quality, agreement and public incidents; risk combines status, incidents, confidence, agreement and how many seeds answered.

Status

Status describes what DNO saw of the public seeds this cycle: whether at least two reported their own height, whether those heights agree, and whether a public incident is active. It does not say whether blocks are being produced (height_static_seconds reports height movement, and status_reason mentions it when status is stable), and it is not a measure of network size or validator count.

stable — At least two public seeds reported their own block height, their heights agree (strong agreement), and no warning or critical public incident is active. One seed not answering does not by itself change status. status_reason says "Heights advancing" only when some seed reported a higher height within the last two observation rounds. When no answering seed has reported a higher height for 5 minutes, it says "height unchanged for N min" and height_static_seconds carries the duration; the cause of a static height is not known from here, so it does not change status. Right after a restart nothing has been compared yet: height_static_seconds is null until the next round, and until DNO observes an advance it counts from the first round that showed the current height (a lower bound).

degraded — Agreement is moderate, or a warning-level public incident is active.

unstable — A critical public incident is active, or agreement is weak.

unknown — The Oracle cannot form a reading. This happens when fewer than two public seeds reported their own block height, or when the last observation is more than 300 seconds old. data_quality_reason names which. This is not a health judgment — it is an explicit statement that the Oracle does not have enough information.

Trend

Trend tells you whether the situation is getting better, holding steady, or getting worse, based on comparing the current observation against recent history. It is purely backward-looking — it describes what has changed, never what will change.

Trend compares the current observation with the average of up to 15 previous observations inside a short recent window, and needs at least 10 of them. After a restart or data gap, trend is unknown until enough recent history accumulates.

Risk

Risk summarises the observed conditions that reduce the margin of this reading. It is a snapshot of the present reading, not a forecast.

low — none of the conditions below. elevated — status degraded or unknown, a warning-level public incident, uncertain confidence, moderate agreement, or more than one configured seed not answering. risk_factors lists the seed, incident and agreement conditions among these. A fault is not confirmed. high — status unstable, a critical public incident, or weak agreement.

When status is unknown, risk defaults to elevated. The Oracle treats the absence of information as a reason for caution — but not alarm. On a testnet where unknown is a normal occurrence, defaulting to high would desensitize consumers.

Data quality

Data quality measures whether DNO got enough to compare: at least two seeds that reported their own height, in an observation at most 300 s old. It describes DNO's vantage, not the network.

sufficient — At least two public seeds reported their own block height in the latest observation, and it is at most 300 s old. insufficient — Fewer seeds answered, fewer reported their own height, or the last completed observation is older than 300 s (data_quality_reason: too_few_answers, too_few_heights, stale, no_observation). When data quality is insufficient, status is set to unknown.

Confidence

Confidence measures whether the heights DNO compared are close to each other. Besides data quality it has one input: the gap between the highest and the lowest own height. DNO can have enough fresh heights (data quality sufficient) and still see them far apart. That is a confidence problem.

clear — the own heights are within 50 blocks of each other. uncertain — they are more than 50 blocks apart, or data quality is insufficient and nothing was compared. Status and risk are still published.

Data quality ≠ confidence. "I can't see enough" is a data quality problem. "What I see is contradictory" is a confidence problem. A reading can have sufficient data quality and uncertain confidence: enough fresh heights, far apart. The reverse does not occur: with insufficient data quality nothing is compared, and confidence is uncertain.

Agreement

Agreement measures whether the heights the public seeds report agree. It is computed only from public seeds that reported their own height: a seed's height is its own peerlist entry. A seed that does not list itself has no height here, and neither does a seed that answers with a key other than the one DNO configured for its URL, since that answer may come from another node. A first listed peer's height stays on its /health row (height_source first_peer) and is counted nowhere: not in total_nodes, aligned_nodes, the median or the spread. Seeds that did not answer, and peer-listed identities (never dialed), do not count as disagreement. Agreement is derived from those heights — specifically, how many are within 25 blocks of the median (the middle height, or the upper of the two middle heights when the count is even).

strong — every own height is within ±25 blocks of the median, and the spread is at most 20 blocks. moderate — at least 60% of them, rounded up (2 of 3), are within ±25 blocks of the median. weak — fewer are. unknown — data quality is insufficient: fewer than two public seeds reported their own height, or the observation is stale (data_quality_reason says which). Heights were not compared (aligned_nodes and block_spread are null).

The Oracle publishes the underlying data alongside the label: aligned node count, total node count, median block height, and block spread. At current network size, calculated percentages would be misleadingly precise, so the Oracle publishes counts instead.

Incidents

DNO keeps a public record of its own reading. When its reading of the public seeds stays unknown (a visibility record), degraded or unstable for 3 consecutive rounds, about a minute, it opens a condition record, and it closes the record after 9 consecutive rounds, about three minutes, without that condition. Severities are fixed: visibility and degraded records are warning, unstable records critical. While visibility is poor, no degraded or unstable record is opened: DNO does not describe a condition it cannot see. One exception: on the first round after a start, if the reading is already unknown and no visibility record is open, DNO opens one at once, dated from the earliest retained round of that unknown run when its history shows it.

Condition records follow status, so they do not feed it: active_incidents and max_incident_severity leave them out, and active_public_conditions counts them. /incidents lists them with kind: "condition", and /timeline shows them. active_incidents counts any other public-scope incident; the current code opens none of those, so it normally reads 0.

The incidents of DNO's operator's own nodes are fleet-scoped: they are left out of every public count, and /incidents serves public scope only.

Records persist across restarts: active rows are reloaded from DNO's database, and the round counts that open and close them start again. A height that stops moving is reported in status_reason and height_static_seconds; it does not change status, so it opens no record by itself.

How It Works

The Oracle runs a continuous observation cycle. Every 20 seconds, it:

1. Fetches each configured public seed's /info in parallel — whether it answered, its own block height, software version and peer count. The same peerlists feed the catalog of peer-listed identities, which are never dialed; a new identity is kept only after two public peerlists have listed it, and one peerlist brings at most 50 new identities into that count per crawl.
2. Computes agreement from the seeds' own block heights.
3. Runs the public state computation, which derives the seven values.
4. Opens or closes DNO's condition records (see Incidents).
5. Updates the endpoints with the new reading.

DAHR (the Demos Data Agnostic HTTPS Relay) is attempted on the cross-check RPCs, not on the seeds whose answers enter status. /health reports it in attestation (available, last_count, last_ok_at); last_count is how many of those relays returned a transaction hash, and /organism carries no attestation. Per the Demos docs, each relay call creates an on-chain web2Request transaction; DNO counts a relay when a transaction hash comes back and does not check that the transaction was included. on_chain_publication covers DNO's own posts: fleet alerts, recoveries and daily summaries, never the public reading. While it is disabled, DNO posts none of them. What one DAHR call records is on About Demos.

On a separate loop, once a minute, DNO also reads the on-chain validators table from the same seeds and dials the address each ACTIVE validator published. Those counts are published on /health and enter none of the seven values: see On-chain Validators.

The public state computation is a single function that takes public node observations as input and produces all seven values as output. Fleet records are filtered out inside it and never shape a public value. The computation is deterministic: the same observations, history, open records and observation age give the same values.

Public vs Reference Separation

This is the most important structural rule in the Oracle. It governs everything.

Public network (primary observation basis)

All top-level assessments — status, trend, risk, data quality, confidence, agreement — are derived exclusively from public network observations. Public assessment uses the configured Kynesys seed URLs. Independent of DNO: true. Independent of each other: false. One org.

Reference fleet (internal context)

The Oracle operator (XM33) also runs a fleet of validator nodes. These provide internal context and infrastructure health monitoring. Fleet data never influences the public assessment and is not part of the public feeds.

The fleet is a reference layer because it is operated by a single entity and may be on a different chain state than the public network; its state does not represent the public network. A fleet node that the public peerlists list appears among the peer-listed identities like any other row, as described under Known Limitations.

How separation is enforced

In the API: the compact agent feed (/organism) contains zero fleet fields. Fleet incidents are tagged with scope "fleet" and excluded from the public incident count. The /incidents endpoint defaults to public scope only.

Fleet views (/dashboard, /history and everything under it) are served on DNO's internal listener only; the public site answers 404 for them.

On-chain Validators

A second question, kept apart from status: how many validators the on-chain validators table lists, and how many of them answer DNO at the seeds' height. DNO answers it with four counts on /health, each inside the one above. None of them enters status, risk, trend, confidence, agreement, incidents or /organism.

The read

Once a minute, on its own loop, DNO sends two read-only calls to the three public seeds, in the Demos SDK's nodeCall format: getValidators, the rows of the validators table still active at the current block, and getNetworkParameters, the parameters in force. The calls go to the address each seed name resolved to, refuse redirects and read at most 2 MB. The rows are reduced on arrival to address, status and published connection URL; stake and timestamps are dropped.

A count is published only when at least two seeds return the same list, meaning the same address–status pairs, and minValidatorStake only when at least two report the same value. When two groups of seeds tie, nothing is published. A seed whose last /info answered with a key other than its configured one is not asked: its answer would be another node's. unstaking counts the rows with status UNSTAKING in that list, and is null when there are none: whether the list carries UNSTAKING rows at all is not confirmed. EXITED validators are not in it.

The watch

DNO then dials GET /info once at the connection URL each ACTIVE row published on chain, as the agreeing seeds list it. The probe rules are the fixnet probe's: only a bare public http origin, connected at the address that was checked, no redirects, at most 2 MB read and a 5 s timeout. The watch adds its own bounds: at most 200 addresses a round, each dialed once, 8 at a time. Published names are looked up 8 at a time; each lookup keeps its place until it ends, 10 s at most, and no new lookup starts after 20 s of a round. Names not looked up are over the round cap. An https address is not dialed: the probe connects to the address it checked, and a certificate names a host. An origin that several ACTIVE rows publish is dialed once, and its answer names one key, so at most one of those rows can answer as its own key. Published origins that resolve to the same address are dialed once between them; each row is still compared with the keys listed on its own published origin.

Rows outside those counts are counted by reason: not dialed (no address published, an address that is not a bare public http origin, a published name that did not resolve to a public address this round, agreeing seeds that list different addresses, or over the round cap), no answer, answered with another key, answered without a key, away from the seeds' height, or answered without its own height. Of the rows answered with another key, other_key_shared counts those whose published origin answered as another key listed on that same origin, and other_key_shared_origins how many origins they sit on: those rows share an address with a key that answered there, which is not the same as not answering. The watch also publishes how many distinct published origins it dialed and the most ACTIVE rows that published one of them, and answered_as_listed_seeds: how many of the rows that answered as their own key are among the three public seeds whose answers enter status. That is an overlap, never added to anything. Versions are grouped from the answers as the listed key, and a version is named only in a release form (such as 0.9.9 or 1.0.0-beta.2) and only when at least two validators answered with it; any other text is counted, never shown.

First counted: DNO's clock

Next to the counts DNO keeps one record: for each key an agreed list showed ACTIVE, the time of the first agreed list that did, on the server's UTC clock. The only other value kept with it is the time of the agreed list before it, which the gap rule below needs: no address, connection URL, stake or protocol time. It says when DNO first counted a key, not when the key staked or the chain admitted it. It sits in the same database as DNO's other observations, so a restart keeps it. From it, on_chain_validators publishes how many ACTIVE keys DNO first counted in the last 24 hours, 7 days and 30 days (first_agreed_today, first_agreed_week, first_agreed_month), only while the list agrees, with first_agreed_since, the time of the record's first list.

  • The record starts at the first agreed list with at least one ACTIVE key. The keys on that list are its starting point and are never counted as new. A window that starts before that list has no figure: it is null, and first_agreed_reason says why. The homepage shows "not reported", never +0.
  • A key first seen after more than an hour without an agreed list (DNO stopped, the seeds disagreed, or a write failed) counts in a window only when the list before that gap is inside it. When the gap crosses the start of a window, that window has no figure: the key's first time is not known.
  • A key that later leaves ACTIVE still counts where it first appeared. The figures are not net of exits, and they are not the number of validators that joined.
  • While a write to the record fails, every figure is null.

The windows run on the server's clock, so a wrong clock moves them. This is not the catalog's first counted, which dates a peer-listed identity from the first public peerlist that listed it, once a second one has. The validators table's own firstSeen field is not used; it has not yet been shown that two seeds return the same value for it.

What the counts do not say

An answer shows that the address a validator published answered DNO, from DNO's vantage, and what that answer said about itself. It does not show that the validator sits in a shard or that consensus uses it. No answer does not change the row on chain: the address may refuse outside callers, or be out of reach from here only. When several keys publish one origin, DNO does not see what stands behind it. If it answers as one of them, the others are counted as sharing that origin with a key that answered, not as silent; if it does not answer, every row on it is no answer. A row that publishes no address has nothing to dial, which says nothing about the validator. "At the seeds' height" is DNO's comparison, not Demos' sync check, and "every round, last hour" is DNO's own record of its rounds, not an uptime figure for the validator.

Unlike peer-listed identities, each of these addresses was published on chain by the validator itself, and the dials run on their own loop, apart from the seed reads that status depends on. /health publishes counts only: never an address, a key, a connection URL, a per-validator height, a first-counted time for any key, or any stake.

Interpretation Model

The Oracle keeps what the seeds showed apart from how much margin the reading has:

• Status describes what the public seeds showed this cycle.
• Risk is a level set by the observed conditions that reduce the margin of that reading; risk_factors names some of them.
• Agreement measures alignment among public seeds that reported their own height.
• Peer-listed identities are never dialed and do not enter status, risk or agreement.
• On-chain validator counts come from the seeds' validator list and DNO's own dials, and do not enter status, risk or agreement either.

No rule changes status because a seed is silent; the agreement of the remaining heights can change it. A silent seed shows in the seeds line and, when more than one is silent, in risk. A stable reading with elevated risk needs more than one configured seed silent while at least two others agree. With the three configured seeds that cannot happen: two silent seeds leave one height, and the reading is unknown.

Explanation Layer

The Oracle provides explanatory fields alongside its categorical assessments. These fields are intended to improve interpretability for human readers and machine consumers.

status_reason

Explains the primary observed basis for the current status. Examples: Heights advancing; public nodes aligned, Public nodes aligned; height unchanged for 12 min, Insufficient data: fewer than 2 public nodes answered.

risk_factors

Lists active observed contributors to current network fragility or reduced resilience. This array may be empty when no material risk factors are currently detected.

agreement_reason

Describes the alignment basis used in the agreement assessment. Example: 3 of 3 public nodes with a height within ±25 blocks of the median (spread: 0 blocks); when heights were not compared: Not compared: fewer than 2 public nodes reported their own block height.

confidence_reason

Explains why confidence is clear or uncertain. Typical values: Observed public signals agree (clear), No cross-check: fewer than 2 public nodes answered (uncertain, insufficient data), or Public nodes report block heights more than 50 blocks apart (uncertain, divergent heights).

These explanatory fields summarize observed conditions. They are not guarantees or operational advice.

Historical Metrics

The Oracle displays rolling historical metrics derived from its own observation cycles over the current history window.

uptime_7d — percentage of observed cycles in the last seven days in which the configured seed answered (seeds only; catalog identities are never dialed).

avg_latency_7d — average response latency across successful observed cycles.

These historical metrics are observational summaries of the configured seeds, not statements about whoever runs them. uptime_7d and avg_latency_7d stay null until more than ten rounds are recorded for a seed. Historical metrics do not change the core assessment model unless explicitly stated.

Rolling observed history. Early values may refine over time.

Last 24 Hours Summary

The "Last 24 Hours" panel on the homepage surfaces aggregate observation from the most recent 24 hours of probe cycles. All fields derive from the public node history table and the public-scope incidents table.

Public nodes observed

How many seeds reported their own height in each round. It reflects what the Oracle observed, not the number of validators in the network. "Typically N" (typical_set_size) is the most common count across the window; "peak M" (peak_set.size) is the largest. Rounds recorded before 1.1 stored a different count (every height a seed returned, or the number of configured seeds when heights were not compared), so for 24 hours after the update the window mixes the two.

Peak observation window

When the typical and peak counts differ, this row reports how often the larger set was assembled and how many seeds answered on average in those rounds. It frames observation quality, not network failure: "3 nodes observed; 3.0 answered on average across 156 cycles" means that in 156 rounds of the last 24 hours three seeds reported their own height, and three answered on average in those rounds. Under the 1.1 rule the average is never below the count, since a seed must answer to report its own height; rounds recorded before 1.1 can make it lower during the first 24 hours after the update.

Chain movement

Categorical: normal, interrupted, or unknown. Computed by bucketing the 24-hour window into 5-minute intervals and checking whether the median of the seeds' own heights at the end of each bucket is higher than at the end of the previous one. Normal requires an advance in at least 95% of the compared buckets and no static run of 5 minutes or more; since one bucket without an advance is already a 5-minute run, every compared bucket must advance. Interrupted fires on either threshold. Unknown indicates fewer than 12 buckets of data — typically during partial windows. blocks_advanced is the newest median minus the oldest, over rounds with sufficient data, as observed; it is null when chain movement is unknown, when there is no pair to compare, when the newest median is below the oldest, or when the median fell by more than 25 blocks between two rounds, and it implies no target rate.

Longest non-stable interval

The longest contiguous run of cycles where the Oracle's computed status was "unstable" or "degraded". Cycles in "unknown" state break the run because "unknown" indicates an observation gap, not network state; so does a gap of more than three rounds without an observation.

Critical public incidents

active_critical_public_incidents counts critical public incidents active now; critical_public_incidents_in_window adds those resolved inside the window. Neither counts fleet incidents or DNO's condition records (active_public_conditions and /incidents report the condition records).

Coverage

The fraction of expected probe cycles actually observed in the window (86,400 seconds / 20-second cadence = 4,320 expected cycles). When coverage is below 50%, sufficient is false and only the coverage figures and a message are published until the rolling window is sufficiently observed.

Scope and Limits

The Oracle is a watch-only observability system. It summarizes observed network conditions and historical measurements but does not provide financial, operational, legal, or governance advice.

A row in DNO's outputs is an observation of an identity or an endpoint, not a statement about whoever runs it.

Oracle outputs are based on externally observable signals available during each measurement cycle. Public endpoint availability, temporary network conditions, or incomplete visibility may affect results. Outputs should be interpreted as structured observed assessments, not guarantees of present or future validator behavior.

Understanding Uncertainty

The Oracle treats uncertainty as a first-class output. On a young testnet with small, evolving node coverage, partial visibility is a normal operating condition — not an error.

The Oracle starts from caution and earns stronger claims through sustained observation. It prefers honest uncertainty over artificial precision.

Three kinds of uncertainty

Insufficient data. The Oracle does not have enough information to form an assessment. Too few public seeds answered, fewer than two reported their own height, or the Oracle just started and hasn't completed a full cycle yet. Effect: data quality → insufficient, status → unknown, risk → elevated.

Stale data. The Oracle has data, but it may be too old to reflect current reality. Observation failures, network timeouts, or process interruptions can cause staleness. The Oracle always publishes how many seconds ago it last observed the network (staleness_seconds) so consumers can apply their own freshness judgment.

Divergent heights. The Oracle has fresh, sufficient data, but the own heights it compared are more than 50 blocks apart. Effect: confidence → uncertain; data quality stays sufficient, and agreement_reason gives the spread. With fewer than two own heights there is nothing to compare: that is insufficient data, not divergence.

The unknown contract

When the Oracle reports unknown, insufficient, or uncertain for any field:

1. The categorical value is stated as a word. Counts that could not be computed are null or left out, never 0 or a guess (before the first observation, staleness_seconds counts from the agent's start).
2. No guess, default, or cached value is substituted.
3. Consumers can rely on these states as stable, intentional signals — not bugs.

Known Limitations

DEMOS is a young testnet. The Oracle is designed for this reality and makes no claims beyond what it can observe.

Small public node set. The Oracle currently reads three public Kynesys seeds. Assessments use coarse-grained categories that match this resolution. As the network grows, finer granularity will be added without structural changes.

Peer-listed identities. The public seeds' peerlists name other identities. DNO keeps each one after two public peerlists have listed it, records its first and last listing, and never dials it. A row is a peerlist observation: not a row of the on-chain validators table (which DNO reads separately, for counts only), not a census of Demos beta, and not a promise of monitoring. The peerlists also carry a second status flag whose meaning DNO has not confirmed; it is not published. In one crawl, one peerlist brings at most 50 identities DNO has never seen into the count; the rest wait for a later crawl. That bounds how fast one peerlist can grow the waiting set or, with a second peerlist, the catalog. The total is bounded by the 2,000-row cap, where rows no public peerlist has listed for 30 days make room.

Why DNO does not dial them. An answer would show only that an address a peer advertised answered DNO: not that the identity is a validator, that it sits in a shard, or that the network has more capacity, and the count would still not be a census. Status has one input, the configured seeds, so answers from peer-listed identities could not enter it without letting whoever controls those listings move it. And the addresses are chosen by the peers: dialing hundreds of them every round, in the loop that reads the seeds, would compete with the seed reads that status depends on.

The catalog lists every identity it kept that is not a configured seed. That includes any node run by DNO's operator that the public peerlists list; such rows are displayed like every other row and are not otherwise identified.

Observable coverage only. The Oracle can only assess what it can reach. Nodes that are firewalled, on private networks, or not yet discovered are invisible. The Oracle is honest about this — unknown and insufficient are always explicitly stated.

No prediction. The Oracle describes what is, not what will be. Even trend is backward-looking. Predictive signals may be added as a separate, clearly labeled layer in the future — never merged into the core assessment model.

DNO informs context; it does not advise, predict, score, certify, or decide action.

A seed that answers with a key other than the one configured for its URL gives no height. Chain movement and the static-height clock use only rounds recorded under the own-height rule. Rows recorded before 1.1, when DNO also read its own node's peerlist, stay hidden until two public peerlists list them, and their first recorded time is then set to the first of those listings. In one crawl, one public peerlist brings at most 50 never-seen identities into the two-peerlist count.

On-chain validators — 2026-09-29. /health gains on_chain_validators (the validators table as the public seeds list it: counts by status and minValidatorStake, each only when two seeds give the same answer) and validator_watch (one /info dial a minute to the address each ACTIVE validator published on chain, counted). Neither enters status or /organism; the /organism contract is unchanged.

How to Use the Data

For builders and operators

The homepage at / shows the live reading: status with its reason, the validators on chain, the five inputs and the one path into status, each seed against the median, the last 24 hours, the peer-listed identities and the probed seeds, incidents, and the API.

The /health endpoint provides full diagnostic data — canonical fields at the top level, then derived detail: public seeds (with height_source), signals, validator_growth (seed counts and peer-listed identities; its online and synced add both populations and are listed in mixed_fields), the attestation state, and the on-chain validator counts (on_chain_validators, validator_watch).

For agents and automated consumers

The /organism endpoint is the primary agent feed. It returns 17 required top-level fields (plus additive 1.1 fields) and no fleet data. Most fields are flat values; risk_factors is an array and last_24h is a small summary object. Every top-level field is always present. The 17 required fields are never null; the additive 1.1 fields (observed_at, data_quality_reason, height_last_advanced_at, height_static_seconds) are null when there is nothing to report.

The simplest consumption pattern is the minimal triple: read status + risk + confidence. These three fields give DNO's reading in three values. Your agent decides what to do with them — the Oracle never tells you.

Before relying on a reading: data_quality says whether DNO had at least two fresh own heights, and staleness_seconds how old the observation is. When data quality is insufficient, status is unknown and risk elevated by design.

For open records: active_incidents and max_incident_severity describe public incidents, and active_public_conditions counts DNO's condition records, without fetching /incidents.
