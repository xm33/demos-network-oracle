What the Oracle Is

The Demos Network Oracle (DNO) is a public observability layer for the Demos blockchain testnet. It reads the network's public seeds from outside, measures whether the heights they report agree, tracks specific problems, and publishes what it finds.

The Oracle is strictly watch-only. It observes, interprets, and summarizes. It does not command validators, does not advise operators, and does not make predictions about what will happen next. It publishes network state as neutral facts. What anyone does with those facts is their decision.

The Oracle is independent. It is not operated by the Demos core team. It does not control any downstream system. Any agent, builder, or person can consume its output and form their own conclusions.

DNO also publishes a versioned Criteria Catalog — the definitions of its observation criteria (C1–C6), available as data at /criteria.json.

What It Measures

Every Oracle assessment is built from seven independent concepts. Each answers exactly one question about the network. No two concepts overlap — if you can express something by combining two other concepts, it is not given its own field.

Status

Status describes current network operability — whether the chain is functioning right now. It is not a direct measure of network size or validator count.

stable — At least two public seeds reported their own block height, their heights agree (strong agreement), and no warning or critical public incident is active. One seed not answering does not by itself change status. status_reason says "Heights advancing" only when some seed reported a higher height within the last two observation rounds. When no answering seed has reported a higher height for 5 minutes, it says "height unchanged for N min" and height_static_seconds carries the duration; the cause of a static height is not known from here, so it does not change status. Right after a restart nothing has been compared yet: height_static_seconds is null until the next round, and until DNO observes an advance it counts from the first round that showed the current height (a lower bound).

degraded — Agreement is moderate, or a warning-level public incident is active.

unstable — A critical public incident is active, or agreement is weak.

unknown — The Oracle cannot determine the network's condition. This happens when fewer than two public seeds answered with a block height, or when the last observation is more than 300 seconds old. data_quality_reason names which. This is not a health judgment — it is an explicit statement that the Oracle does not have enough information.

Trend

Trend tells you whether the situation is getting better, holding steady, or getting worse, based on comparing the current observation against recent history. It is purely backward-looking — it describes what has changed, never what will change.

Trend compares the current observation with the average of up to 15 previous observations inside a short recent window, and needs at least 10 of them. After a restart or data gap, trend is unknown until enough recent history accumulates.

Risk

Risk describes how dangerous the current observed state is. It is a snapshot — it characterizes the present moment, not a forecast.

low — No significant exposure. elevated — Reduced margin observed (see risk_factors). A fault is not confirmed. high — The network is materially impaired.

When status is unknown, risk defaults to elevated. The Oracle treats the absence of information as a reason for caution — but not alarm. On a testnet where unknown is a normal occurrence, defaulting to high would desensitize consumers.

Data quality

Data quality measures whether the Oracle can see the network properly. It answers: did enough public nodes respond, recently enough, to form a credible assessment?

sufficient — At least two public seeds reported their own block height in the latest observation, and it is at most 300 s old. insufficient — Fewer seeds answered, fewer reported their own height, or the last completed observation is older than 300 s (data_quality_reason: too_few_answers, too_few_heights, stale, no_observation). When data quality is insufficient, status is set to unknown.

Confidence

Confidence measures whether the observed signals agree with each other. The Oracle may have full visibility (data quality is sufficient) but see contradictory information — nodes reporting divergent block heights, mixed health signals. That is a confidence problem.

clear — Signals are consistent and unambiguous. uncertain — Signals conflict or are ambiguous. Status and risk are still published, but should be consumed with caution.

Data quality ≠ confidence. "I can't see enough" is a data quality problem. "What I see is contradictory" is a confidence problem. A network can have sufficient data quality but uncertain confidence (full visibility, conflicting data) — or insufficient data quality but clear confidence (limited data, but what's visible is consistent).

Agreement

Agreement measures whether the heights the public seeds report agree. It is computed only from public seeds that reported their own height: a seed's height is its own peerlist entry. A seed that does not list itself has no height here. Its first listed peer's height stays on its /health row (height_source first_peer) and is counted nowhere: not in total_nodes, aligned_nodes, the median or the spread. Seeds that did not answer, and peer-listed identities (never dialed), do not count as disagreement. Agreement is derived from those heights — specifically, how many are within 25 blocks of the median (the middle height, or the upper of the two middle heights when the count is even).

strong — All or nearly all observed public nodes are aligned. moderate — Most agree, but some divergence exists. weak — Significant divergence. unknown — Fewer than two public seeds reported their own height; heights were not compared (aligned_nodes and block_spread are null).

The Oracle publishes the underlying data alongside the label: aligned node count, total node count, median block height, and block spread. At current network size, calculated percentages would be misleadingly precise, so the Oracle publishes counts instead.

Incidents

Incidents are discrete, named events — a specific node going offline, a chain stall, a detected partition. They have a severity (info, warning, critical) and a lifecycle (opened → active → resolved).

General conditions like "the network is slow" are not incidents — they are captured by status, risk, and agreement. Incidents point to specific, identifiable problems.

Severity is assessed relative to current network size. One node going offline in a set of five is more significant than in a set of fifty.

Incidents are state-driven and persist across restarts. The Oracle reconciles each active incident against current observations every cycle, rather than relying only on transition events. A consequence: an incident may remain active for extended periods when the underlying condition does not resolve — for example, a block-stall may be detected and kept active even while all nodes remain individually healthy and reachable. "All nodes healthy" does not always mean "the network is producing blocks."

How It Works

The Oracle runs a continuous observation cycle. Every 20 seconds, it:

1. Fetches each configured public seed's /info in parallel — whether it answered, its own block height, software version and peer count. The same peerlists feed the catalog of peer-listed identities, which are never dialed; a new identity is kept only after two public peerlists have listed it.
2. Computes agreement from the seeds' own block heights.
3. Checks for active incidents — new problems, resolved problems.
4. Runs the public state computation — derives all seven concepts from the raw observations.
5. Updates all API endpoints with the new state.

DAHR (the Demos Data Agnostic HTTPS Relay) is attempted on the cross-check RPCs, not on the seeds whose answers enter status. /health reports it in attestation (available, last_count, last_ok_at); /organism carries no attestation. While on_chain_publication is disabled, nothing is posted on chain. What one DAHR call records is on About Demos.

The public state computation is a single function that takes public node observations as input and produces all seven values as output. Fleet data never enters this function. The computation is deterministic — given the same observations, it always produces the same assessment.

Public vs Reference Separation

This is the most important structural rule in the Oracle. It governs everything.

Public network (primary observation basis)

All top-level assessments — status, trend, risk, data quality, confidence, agreement — are derived exclusively from public network observations. Public assessment uses the configured Kynesys seed URLs. Independent of DNO: true. Independent of each other: false. One org.

Reference fleet (internal context)

The Oracle operator (XM33) also runs a fleet of validator nodes. These provide internal context and infrastructure health monitoring. Fleet data never influences the public assessment and is not part of the public feeds.

The fleet is a reference layer because it is operated by a single entity, may be on a different chain state than the public network, and its health does not represent public network health. Fleet nodes that appear in public crawl output are additionally listed in the discovered table, resolved identically to other discovered peers, as described under Discovered but unmonitored validators.

How separation is enforced

In the API: the compact agent feed (/organism) contains zero fleet fields. Fleet incidents are tagged with scope "fleet" and excluded from the public incident count. The /incidents endpoint defaults to public scope only.

Fleet views (/dashboard, /history and everything under it) are served on DNO's internal listener only; the public site answers 404 for them.

Interpretation Model

The Oracle separates operability from resilience:

• Status describes whether the chain is functioning now.
• Risk describes how fragile that observed state is.
• Agreement measures alignment among public seeds that reported their own height.
• Peer-listed identities are never dialed and do not enter status, risk or agreement.

This prevents low public node reachability from being misread as immediate chain instability. A network can be stable with elevated risk — meaning: the chain works, but resilience is reduced.

Explanation Layer

The Oracle provides explanatory fields alongside its categorical assessments. These fields are intended to improve interpretability for human readers and machine consumers.

status_reason

Explains the primary observed basis for the current status. Examples: Heights advancing; public nodes aligned, Public nodes aligned; height unchanged for 12 min, Insufficient data: fewer than 2 public nodes answered.

risk_factors

Lists active observed contributors to current network fragility or reduced resilience. This array may be empty when no material risk factors are currently detected.

agreement_reason

Describes the alignment basis used in the agreement assessment. Example: 3 of 3 public nodes with a height within ±25 blocks of the median (spread: 0 blocks); when heights were not compared: Not compared: fewer than 2 public nodes reported their own block height.

confidence_reason

Explains why confidence is clear or uncertain. Typical values: Observed public signals agree (clear), No cross-check: fewer than 2 public nodes answered (uncertain, insufficient data), or Public nodes report block heights more than 50 blocks apart (uncertain, divergent heights).

These explanatory fields summarize current observed conditions. They do not constitute guarantees, certification, or operational advice.

Historical Metrics

The Oracle displays rolling historical metrics derived from its own observation cycles over the current history window.

uptime_7d — percentage of observed cycles in the last seven days in which the configured seed answered (seeds only; catalog identities are never dialed).

avg_latency_7d — average response latency across successful observed cycles.

These historical metrics are observational summaries only. They do not constitute endorsement, certification, or recommendation of any validator or operator. Early values should be interpreted cautiously until sufficient history has accumulated. Historical metrics do not change the core assessment model unless explicitly stated.

Rolling observed history. Early values may refine over time.

Last 24 Hours Summary

The "Last 24 Hours" panel on the homepage surfaces aggregate observation from the most recent 24 hours of probe cycles. All fields derive from the public node history table and the public-scope incidents table.

Public nodes observed

The number of public nodes that returned a usable response in each probe cycle. This reflects what the Oracle observed in probe cycles, not the total number of validators in the network. "Typically N" is the most common observed count across the window. "Peak M" is the largest count observed at any point.

Peak observation window

When the typical and peak counts differ, this row reports how often the larger set was assembled and how many of those nodes answered on average. It frames observation quality, not network failure: "5 nodes observed; 1.0 answered on average across 156 cycles" means the Oracle managed to assemble a 5-node observation 156 times in 24 hours, and during those cycles, on average one of the five answered.

Chain movement

Categorical: normal, interrupted, or unknown. Computed by bucketing the 24-hour window into 5-minute intervals and checking whether the median block number advanced within each bucket. Normal requires advance in at least 95% of buckets and no static run of 5+ minutes. Interrupted fires on either threshold. Unknown indicates fewer than 12 buckets of data — typically during partial windows.

Longest non-stable interval

The longest contiguous run of cycles where the Oracle's computed status was "unstable" or "degraded". Cycles in "unknown" state break the run because "unknown" indicates an observation gap, not network state.

Active critical public incidents

Critical-severity incidents that were open at any point during the window and affect at least one non-fleet node. Fleet-internal incidents are excluded.

Coverage

The fraction of expected probe cycles actually observed in the window (86,400 seconds / 20-second cadence = 4,320 expected cycles). When coverage drops below 50%, all numerical fields are hidden until the rolling window is sufficiently observed.

Scope and Limits

The Oracle is a watch-only observability system. It summarizes observed network conditions and historical measurements but does not provide financial, operational, legal, or governance advice.

Inclusion of a validator in Oracle outputs does not imply endorsement, approval, or certification.

Oracle outputs are based on externally observable signals available during each measurement cycle. Public endpoint availability, temporary network conditions, or incomplete visibility may affect results. Outputs should be interpreted as structured observed assessments, not guarantees of present or future validator behavior.

Understanding Uncertainty

The Oracle treats uncertainty as a first-class output. On a young testnet with small, evolving node coverage, partial visibility is a normal operating condition — not an error.

The Oracle starts from caution and earns stronger claims through sustained observation. It prefers honest uncertainty over artificial precision.

Three kinds of uncertainty

Insufficient data. The Oracle does not have enough information to form an assessment. Too few public nodes responded, or the Oracle just started and hasn't completed a full cycle yet. Effect: data quality → insufficient, status → unknown, risk → elevated.

Stale data. The Oracle has data, but it may be too old to reflect current reality. Observation failures, network timeouts, or process interruptions can cause staleness. The Oracle always publishes how many seconds ago it last observed the network (staleness_seconds) so consumers can apply their own freshness judgment.

Contradictory data. The Oracle has fresh, sufficient data, but the signals conflict — nodes report different block heights, mixed health signals. This is often the most informative kind of uncertainty, because it frequently signals a real network event. A related variant: when only one public node is reachable, confidence is also uncertain — not because signals conflict, but because there is no second source to cross-check against. Effect: confidence → uncertain, but data quality remains sufficient. The problem is in the network or in coverage, not in the Oracle's ability to observe.

The unknown contract

When the Oracle reports unknown, insufficient, or uncertain for any field:

1. The value is explicitly stated — never omitted, never null.
2. No guess, default, or cached value is substituted.
3. Consumers can rely on these states as stable, intentional signals — not bugs.

Known Limitations

DEMOS is a young testnet. The Oracle is designed for this reality and makes no claims beyond what it can observe.

Small public node set. The Oracle currently reads three public Kynesys seeds. Assessments use coarse-grained categories that match this resolution. As the network grows, finer granularity will be added without structural changes.

Peer-listed identities. The public seeds' peerlists name other identities. DNO keeps each one after two public peerlists have listed it, records its first and last listing, and never dials it. A row is a peerlist observation: not a row of the on-chain validators table (DNO does not read it), not a census of Demos beta, and not a promise of monitoring. The peerlists also carry a second status flag whose meaning DNO has not confirmed; it is not published.

The discovered table lists every peer observed in crawl output that is not one of the monitored public nodes shown above it. This includes any peers operated by the oracle's operator; such peers are resolved and displayed identically to all other discovered peers, and are not otherwise identified.

Observable coverage only. The Oracle can only assess what it can reach. Nodes that are firewalled, on private networks, or not yet discovered are invisible. The Oracle is honest about this — unknown and insufficient are always explicitly stated.

No prediction. The Oracle describes what is, not what will be. Even trend is backward-looking. Predictive signals may be added as a separate, clearly labeled layer in the future — never merged into the core assessment model.

DNO informs context; it does not advise, predict, score, certify, or decide action.

How to Use the Data

For builders and operators

The homepage at / shows the live reading: status with its reason, the four inputs and the one path into status, each seed against the median, the last 24 hours, the peer-listed identities and the probed seeds, incidents, and the API.

The /health endpoint provides full diagnostic data — canonical fields at the top level, then derived detail: public seeds (with height_source), signals, validator_growth (seed counts and peer-listed identities; its online and synced add both populations and are listed in mixed_fields), and the attestation state.

For agents and automated consumers

The /organism endpoint is the primary agent feed. It returns 17 required top-level fields (plus additive 1.1 fields) and no fleet data. Most fields are flat values; risk_factors is an array and last_24h is a small summary object. Every top-level field is always present. The 17 required fields are never null; the additive 1.1 fields (observed_at, data_quality_reason, height_last_advanced_at, height_static_seconds) are null when there is nothing to report.

The simplest consumption pattern is the minimal triple: read status + risk + confidence. These three fields give you the complete network picture in three values. Your agent decides what to do with them — the Oracle never tells you.

For trust calibration: check data_quality before relying on the assessment. Check staleness_seconds to know how fresh the data is. If data quality is insufficient, treat all other values with skepticism.

For incident awareness: active_incidents gives the count, max_incident_severity tells you the worst active problem without needing to fetch the full incident list.
